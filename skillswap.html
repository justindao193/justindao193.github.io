The problem

Starting a hobby costs money, and old supplies sit in drawers

Crochet needs hooks and yarn. Painting needs paints and brushes. People with leftovers rarely pass them on, and people starting out pay full price. Our survey of 42 people showed both sides.

The solution

SkillSwap, a local app where swapping comes before buying

People list supplies they no longer use and trade them with someone nearby. If nobody has what they want, they can still buy cheaply. Every step is built to feel safe with a stranger.

01browseFind supplies by hobby

Pick a hobby, set how far you will travel, and see what people nearby are ready to pass on.

02offerMake an offer in two steps

Choose what you give, where and when to meet, then check it all before you send.

03confirmBoth people confirm

A swap only counts when both tap “We swapped!”, then each person leaves a rating.

Ideas to paper

Three concepts, merged into one, then tested on paper

Testers liked the trust score in the social concept but said the trading app felt like Facebook Marketplace. We kept trading at the centre, added community trust, and ran 5 expert reviews and 4 think-alouds on paper.

Fast search and trade requests. Felt too much like existing marketplaces.

Added a forum per hobby. The forums did not help anyone find supplies.

Final design

I redesigned SkillSwap on my own around what testers struggled with

Each clip is the live prototype playing itself. The reasons behind each change follow below.

01browse

Find supplies by hobby

Tap a hobby tag, or open “All categories”. Every card shows the name, then the price or “swap”.

02offer

Make an offer in two steps

Pick what you give, a public spot and a day, then check the whole trade before sending.

03confirm

Accept, renegotiate or decline

After the hand-off, both people tap “We swapped!” and rate each other.

04post

Post with a live photo

The camera opens straight away, so every listing shows the real item.

The rest of the app

My decisions

Why I made these calls

Every change started with something a tester did or said. On the left is the Figma prototype we tested, on the right my redesign.

→
01

Give requests their own screen

Saw
People were unsure what accepting or renegotiating in the chat would do.
Did
A request screen that shows what you get, what you give, where and when, then Accept swap, Renegotiate or Decline. The chat stays for talking.
→
02

Sort by hobby, not by store aisle

Saw
“The home page is messy; categories are not clear.” Testers also asked for hobbies beyond arts and crafts.
Did
Hobby tags on home, 11 groups under “All categories”, and filters for condition, distance and swap or buy.
→
03

Pay only after a yes

Saw
Payment details were asked for before the seller had even accepted.
Did
A two-step offer: pick your item, a public spot and a day, then review. Nothing is paid until the other person accepts.
→
04

Make strangers easier to trust

Saw
In our survey, 60% worried about the other person and 65% about the condition of used supplies.
Did
A live-photo label, condition up front, a tappable rating with reviews, and a reminder to meet in public.

How I built it

I built the redesign as working code with Claude

Instead of making more Figma screens, I described each screen and flow to Claude, which wrote the code. I reviewed every screen and asked for changes until it fit what testers needed.

Claude did

  • Wrote the HTML, CSS and JavaScript
  • Turned my notes into screens and flows
  • Made the self-playing clips above

I decided

  • Which tester problems to fix first
  • Patterns to borrow from eBay, Kijiji and Facebook Marketplace
  • Colours, spacing and every label, like “swapped” instead of “traded”

I pushed back on

  • A blue that did not fit the brand, replaced with dark buttons and pastels
  • A sign-up step, since testers wanted to browse before making an account
  • Crowded screens, split into fewer choices per step

Reflection

What I learned

Looking back, the biggest lessons were about scope and clarity, not screens.

01

Narrow the scope early

Trading, forums and tutorials→Trading done well

We were told early that the scope was too big. Cutting sooner would have left more time for the core flow.

02

Push ideas further apart

Three similar concepts→Truly different directions

Peers said our concepts looked like Facebook Marketplace. Wider brainstorming would have helped.

03

Labels must say what happens

“Accept” in a chat→“Accept swap” on the request

The same confusion came back in two rounds of testing until the action had its own screen.

04

Pick metrics that fit the task

Time and error rate→SUS plus clear task goals

Our open task made times vary from 2 to 8 minutes, and we had not defined what counts as an error.
