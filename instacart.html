    Helping shoppers find familiar groceries and discover new favourites

    Could recommendations help shoppers find familiar groceries with less searching? I defined the scope, compared methods using public Instacart orders and built a working shopping prototype. I chose a simple rule that favours recent purchases, then added a separate discovery list.

    The problem

    Shoppers rebuy the same groceries, but still have to find them

    A visible list could help returning shoppers reach familiar groceries with less searching. Historical orders let me test prediction accuracy; testing with people is needed to assess shopping effort.

    Scope

    What I chose to build, and what I left out

    I started with five familiar products and reversible controls. Discovery came later as a separate feature; prices, stock and checkout stayed outside scope.

    In Version 1
    • Up to five familiar products
    • Add with one tap, never automatically
    • Hide one exact product, then Show it again later
    • Fewer than five when history is short, never random filler
    • Popular products as a clear fallback for a new shopper
    Left out
    • New-product discovery (tested later in Version 3)
    • Prices, stock and checkout
    • Real accounts or an Instacart connection
    • Learning from clicks in real time
    • A separate dashboard

    How I tested

    Use earlier orders to predict a later basket

    Every method faced the same 131,209 shoppers. Lists used earlier purchases only and were saved before checking final baskets.

    Recall measures how much of the next basket the list found. Precision measures how many suggestions matched. Hit rate asks whether at least one matched.

    Methods

    Start simple, then test the extra complexity

    I compared three simple rules and two learned models. Recent purchases earned more weight in the rule I selected.

    1. 1

      General popularity

      The same popular products for everyone.

    2. 2

      Personal frequency

      The products each shopper bought most often.

    3. 3

      Weighted recency

      Add purchase points, giving recent orders more weight.

    4. 4

      Logistic regression

      Learn how to combine five purchase-history clues.

    5. 5

      Boosted trees

      Use small decision trees to combine those clues more flexibly.

    Results

    Personal history made the biggest difference

    The method I chose found about a quarter of each shopper’s next basket. About 4 in 5 shoppers bought at least one of its five suggestions. Showing everyone the same popular products found less than 1 in 20.

    MethodRecall@5Precision@5Hit rate@5
    General popularity4.69%9.48%36.24%
    Personal frequency23.12%35.41%78.23%
    Weighted recency chosen24.28%37.32%79.37%
    Logistic regression24.28%37.30%79.38%
    Boosted trees24.45%37.58%79.45%

    My decision

    I chose a simpler method, even though another scored slightly better

    Boosted trees scored 24.45% recall; weighted recency scored 24.28%. I chose the simpler rule after validation, before the final check, accepting about 0.18 percentage points less recall.

    Easy to trace

    Each earlier purchase contributes a visible number of points.

    Less to maintain

    The default uses a fixed rule rather than a separate trained model.

    An honest trade-off

    Trees were more accurate. Both were fast locally, so speed did not decide it.

    Version 2 · An idea I stopped

    Purchase timing added too little

    I gave extra points when a product looked due, based on its usual purchase gaps. The recall gain was 0.025 percentage points against a 0.25-point criterion fixed beforehand. I stopped this attempt instead of moving the goalposts.

    −0.050Bar to clear: +0.25

    +0.025 points. Its 95% range (−0.003 to +0.052) even includes zero.

    Version 3 · Discovery

    A separate list for something unfamiliar

    I used shared-buyer patterns to rank products absent from each shopper’s recorded history. This keeps discovery separate from buying familiar products again.

    1. 1

      Start with familiar products

      Take up to ten products, weighted by recent purchases.

    2. 2

      Find product connections

      Look for shared buyers, adjusted for each product’s popularity.

    3. 3

      Add personal contributions

      Multiply each familiar product’s weight by its connection strength.

    4. 4

      Remove familiar candidates

      Rank the unfamiliar products; popularity fills any empty slots.

    Historical comparison · 15,000 shoppers
    Popular products9.31%
    Related products10.77%

    Share of shoppers with at least one match among five suggestions. Related products reached a net 219 more shoppers; most still had no match.

    With five suggestions, related products matched purchases for about 11 in 100 shoppers versus 9 for popularity. These shoppers’ records had already been reviewed, so new data would be the real check.

    What I learned

    Good scores are only part of a useful product

    Three decisions shaped the outcome.

    Choose deliberatelyAccept a small accuracy cost

    I chose an inspectable rule while keeping the stronger tree result visible.

    Stop with evidenceDo not move the goalposts

    I stopped timing when it missed the fixed criteria, then explored discovery as a different question.

    Keep claims separatePrediction is not product value

    Historical matches supported the prototype. Only better user testing can establish shopping benefit.
